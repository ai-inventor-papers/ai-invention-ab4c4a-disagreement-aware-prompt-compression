# Q2: Verbalized sentence-importance elicitation without index/position/count bias

## 2a. How existing LLM sentence-compressors elicit/present sentences, and what goes wrong

**LLMLingua-2 (2403.12968).** The GPT-4 distillation instruction is given verbatim in the paper:

> "Compress the given text to short expressions, and such that you (GPT-4) can reconstruct it as
> close as possible to the original. Unlike the usual text compression, I need you to comply with
> the 5 conditions below: 1. You can ONLY remove unimportant words. 2. Do not reorder the original
> words. 3. Do not change the original words. 4. Do not use abbreviations or emojis. 5. Do not add
> new words or symbols. Compress the origin aggressively by removing words only. Compress the
> origin as short as you..." (p.4, "Our Instruction for Compression")

Long contexts are **chunked at ≤512 tokens, ending with a period**, before each chunk is
compressed independently: "we first segment each long context into multiple chunks, each
containing no more than 512 tokens and ending with a period. We then instruct GPT-4 to compress
each chunk individually." Labelling problems are explicit: GPT‑4 "tends to modify expressions used
in the original texts and sometimes generates hallucinated content," motivating the **Variation
Rate (VR)** quality-control filter: "VR measures the proportion of words in the compressed text
that are [not in the original]... A higher variation rate implies a higher likelihood of
encountering hallucinated content. Therefore, we exclude the examples with the top 5% highest
variation rates." [VERIFIED, arXiv:2403.12968]

**Selective Context (2310.06201)** scores lexical units by **self-information** (surprisal) from a
base causal LM, not by an LLM verbal judgment: "Selective Context evaluates informativeness of
lexical units (i.e., tokens, phrases, or sentences) with self-information (Shannon, 1948) computed
by a base causal language model. By selectively retaining content with higher self-information,
our method provides a more compact and efficient context representation." And: "self-information
can be used to assess the informativeness of lexical units, e.g., words, phrases, or sentences.
Lexical units with lower self-information are less informative... we may treat these parts of
input as redundant." This is a **numeric, model-probability-based** score, not a verbalized
list/index elicitation — it sidesteps position bias entirely by construction. [VERIFIED,
arXiv:2310.06201]

**RECOMP (2310.04408)**, extractive compressor: sentences are not presented to a generative LLM as
a numbered list at inference time; instead a **trained dual encoder** embeds each candidate
sentence sᵢ and the query x, and picks by inner-product similarity/contrastive score: "Given n
sentences [s1, s2...sn] in the input document set..., we train a dual encoder model encθ which
embeds sentence si and the input sequence x into fixed-dimensional embeddings respectively. Their
inner product represents how helpful it would be for the LM M to prepend si to the input x to
generate y." An **oracle** variant is also defined by brute-force substitution: "an extractive
oracle which selects a sentence in evidence documents that leads to the best task performance."
This is effectively a leave-in-one-sentence (single-sentence) oracle, structurally close to what
Q4 needs. [VERIFIED, arXiv:2310.04408]

**CompAct (2407.09014)** does use an LLM to pick sentences, and its prompt is given: "1. Choose 0
to 3 sentences that directly address the critical points needed to answer the question.
Additionally, include 0 to 3 sentences that provide useful context, even if they do not directly
address the question. Ensure that you avoid selec..." (Table 11, prompt template with placeholders
`[SOURCE SENTENCES]`, `[REFERENCE SENTENCES]`, `[QUESTION]`). This is a **soft, non-exact-count**
selection band (0–3 + 0–3), not a fixed K, and sentences are referenced by their content/position
in an iterative multi-round summarization loop rather than by an explicit `S1..Sn` id scheme.
[VERIFIED, arXiv:2407.09014]

**Search for 2025–2026 "LLM selects sentences" / self-summarization compressors:** scholarly
(OpenAlex) and general searches for "LLM self-summarization sentence selection compressor 2025"
and "arxiv 2025 sentence selection context compression LLM select sentences prompt" returned only
tangential survey papers (RAG surveys, LLM-for-SE surveys) — no new 2025/2026 paper doing exactly
"LLM verbally selects sentence ids" was found in the time box. **UNVERIFIED / none found** for
this specific sub-bullet; the closest and best-verified prior art for the exact "select sentence
ids from a numbered list" pattern is Referential Dangling's discussion of hard compressors
(§2b/Q4 below) and CompAct's sentence-level selection step above.

## 2b. Known biases in LLM list/index/position selection

**Position bias in pairwise LLM evaluation — Wang et al. 2023, "Large Language Models are not Fair
Evaluators" (2305.17926).** Quote: "This manipulation allows us to skew the evaluation result,
making one model appear considerably superior to the other, e.g., Vicuna-13B could beat ChatGPT on
66 over 80 tested queries with ChatGPT as an evaluator. To address this issue, we propose a
calibration framework with three simple yet effective strategies: 1) Multiple Evidence
Calibration... 2) Balanced Position Calibration, which aggregates results across various orders to
determine the final score; 3) Human-in-the-Loop Calibration..." Balanced Position Calibration
itself: "we evaluate each candidate in both positions across two runs and compute the final score
as the average of the two runs." [VERIFIED, arXiv:2305.17926] — this is the direct precedent for
our "repeat with shuffled order, keep only if selected in both" stability rule.

**Option-ID token bias — Zheng et al. 2023, "Large Language Models Are Not Robust Multiple Choice
Selectors" (2309.03882).** Quote: "modern LLMs are vulnerable to option position changes in MCQs
due to their inherent 'selection bias', namely, they prefer to select specific option IDs as
answers (like 'Option A')... this behavioral bias primarily stems from LLMs' token bias, where the
model a priori assigns more probabilistic mass to specific option ID tokens (e.g., A/B/C/D)... To
mitigate selection bias, we propose... PriDe, which separates the model's prior bias for option IDs
from the overall prediction distribution." **Magnitude of the shift** (quoted, MMLU 0‑shot,
answer-moving attack): "moving the golden answers to position D degrades the accuracy of
gpt-3.5-turbo by 6.3 (from 67.2 to 60.9). When moving to A, llama-30b is boosted by 15.2 and
surpasses gpt-3.5-turbo (68.2 vs. 65.3)." Also: "llama-30B selects A/B/C/D 34.6% / 27.3% / 22.3% /
15.8% of the time" on a class-balanced sample — a strongly nonuniform (χ² p≪10⁻⁴) selection
distribution over option identity alone. [VERIFIED, arXiv:2309.03882] This directly motivates using
fresh, content-blind ids (S1..Sn) plus permutation control rather than trusting raw id-selection
frequency.

**Primacy/recency — "Lost in the Middle" (2307.03172).** Quote: "we observe a distinctive
U-shaped performance curve... language model performance is highest when relevant information
occurs at the very beginning (primacy bias) or end of its input context (recency bias), and
performance significantly degrades when models must access and use information in the middle of
their input context." Table 1 quantifies closed-book vs. oracle accuracy (used again in Q4c).
[VERIFIED, arXiv:2307.03172]

**RankGPT (2304.09542)** demonstrates LLMs (ChatGPT/GPT-4) as zero-shot listwise re-rankers:
"properly instructed LLMs can deliver competitive, even superior results to state-of-the-art
supervised methods on popular IR benchmarks... we delve into the potential for distilling the
ranking capabilities of ChatGPT into small specialized models using a permutation distillation
scheme." [VERIFIED, arXiv:2304.09542] It establishes the sliding-window listwise-ranking prompt
paradigm that "permutation self-consistency" (below) is built to de-bias.

**Permutation self-consistency — Tang et al. 2023, "Found in the Middle" (2310.07712, verified
correct arXiv id).** Quote: "Large language models (LLMs) exhibit positional bias in how they use
context, which especially affects listwise ranking. To address this, we propose permutation
self-consistency, a form of self-consistency over the ranking list outputs of black-box LLMs. Our
key idea is to marginalize out different list orders in the prompt to produce an order-independent
ranking with less positional bias." Effect size: "consistently increasing the scores of GPT-3.5,
GPT-4, and LLaMA v2 (70B...) by up to 4–17%, 9–24%, and 8–16%, respectively." Diminishing-returns
note: "the score improvement from using five aggregated rankings reaches 67% of twenty, on
average, suggesting that a few suffice for quality gain." [VERIFIED, arXiv:2310.07712] This is the
direct precedent for our shuffle-and-remap CONTROL protocol below (2c).

**Round-number / fixed-count bias in LLM item selection:** targeted search ("round number bias LLM
item selection fixed count") did not surface a paper specifically about LLMs preferring
round/fixed counts of selected items; it surfaced adjacent work — "When Can You Debias an LLM
Judge? Identifiability Limits... for Top-k Ranking" (arXiv 2607.02104) and "Unveiling Selection
Biases: Exploring Order and Token Sensitivity in Large Language Models" (ACL Findings 2024) — both
on option/order sensitivity, not specifically round-number counts. **UNVERIFIED** for a
round-number-specific citation; flagged as an open risk that our protocol should test empirically
(hence the fixed, task-derived K = round(budget_ratio·n) rather than letting the model choose a
count).

**Lead bias in extractive summarization — Kedzie et al. 2018, "Content Selection in Deep Learning
Models of Summarization" (1810.12343).** Quote: "Sentence position bias dominates the learning
signal for news summarization, though not for other domains." And on the mechanism: "Sentence
position is a well known and powerful feature for news summarization..., owing to the intentional
lead bias in the news article writing; it also explains the difficulty in beating the lead baseline
for single-document summarization." Shuffle-ablation result: "there is a significant drop in
performance when the document order is shuffled... This suggest[s] that position is being learned
by the models in the news/journal article domain even when the model has no explicit position
features." Also: "Shuffling reduces lead overlap to 35.2% but the overall system performance drops
significantly; the models are not able to identify important information without position."
[VERIFIED, arXiv:1810.12343] This is the precedent for our "no-lead control" (2c): position-1
sentences are disproportionately selected regardless of content, so disagreement rate should be
reported with sentence-1-of-paragraph excluded.

## 2c. Recommended elicitation format

Two prompt variants below assume the passage has already been split into a list of sentences and
assigned fresh, randomized ids `S1..Sn` (never the original document order on the first pass — see
CONTROL). `n` = number of sentences, `budget_ratio` = target fraction to keep, `K =
round(budget_ratio * n)`.

**Variant A — task-agnostic (question not shown).**
```
You will be given a passage split into numbered sentences. Your job is to select the sentences
that carry the passage's most important, load-bearing factual content — the sentences a reader
would need to reconstruct the key facts and their relationships, independent of any specific
question.

Passage title: {TITLE}

S1: {sentence 1}
S2: {sentence 2}
...
Sn: {sentence n}

Select exactly {K} sentence ids that together best preserve the passage's important information.
Do not explain your reasoning. Output only JSON of the form {"keep": ["S3", "S7", ...]} and
nothing else — no prose, no markdown fences.
```

**Variant B — query-aware (question shown).**
```
You will be given a question and a passage split into numbered sentences. Your job is to select
the sentences needed to answer the question, or that state entities/facts the answer sentence
depends on (e.g. bridge entities).

Question: {QUESTION}
Passage title: {TITLE}

S1: {sentence 1}
S2: {sentence 2}
...
Sn: {sentence n}

Select exactly {K} sentence ids that are necessary to answer the question. Do not explain your
reasoning. Output only JSON of the form {"keep": ["S3", "S7", ...]} and nothing else — no prose,
no markdown fences.
```

Execution parameters: temperature = 0; strict JSON parse; on parse failure or a `keep` list whose
length ≠ K or whose ids are not a subset of `{S1..Sn}`, issue exactly one re-prompt appending "Your
previous answer was invalid because {reason}. Reply again with only the JSON object." If the
re-prompt also fails, mark the item as a parse failure (excluded from the stability analysis, not
silently coerced).

**CONTROL (permutation check, following Tang et al. 2023 / Wang et al. 2023 above).** Run the same
prompt twice per passage: once with sentences in original document order and fresh ids `S1..Sn`
assigned in that order, and once with the sentence *contents* randomly permuted and fresh ids
`S1..Sn` re-assigned to the shuffled order. Map both selections back to original sentence indices.
Report:
- **Jaccard(original, shuffled)** = |kept_orig ∩ kept_shuf| / |kept_orig ∪ kept_shuf|, per passage
  and averaged.
- **Per-original-position selection frequency**: for each original position p ∈ {1..n}, the
  fraction of (ordering × repeat) conditions in which the sentence at that original position was
  kept — this exposes primacy/recency/id-token bias (2b) directly as a function over position,
  analogous to Fig. 1 of Lost in the Middle.

**STABILITY RULE.** A sentence is **kept** in the final selection only if it was selected in
*both* orderings (original and shuffled) after mapping back to original indices. A sentence
selected in exactly one ordering is **UNSTABLE** and reported/analysed separately (candidate list
for manual audit or a tie-break rule), never silently included or excluded.

**No-lead control.** Following Kedzie et al. 2018 (lead bias), compute the disagreement rate
(1 − per-position stability, i.e. fraction UNSTABLE) twice: once over all sentences, and once
excluding sentence 1 of each paragraph. A materially lower disagreement rate on the full set than
on the no-lead subset indicates the elicitation format is still leaning on lead position rather
than content, and should be reported as a caveat rather than fixed silently in this study.

## 2d. Two-round self-critique protocol

**Round 1** = the elicitation above (Variant A or B), producing a `keep` set and therefore a
`dropped` set = `{S1..Sn} \ keep`.

**Round 2 prompt** (shown only the dropped sentences, one at a time, never the kept set, per spec):
```
Passage title: {TITLE}

The following sentence was removed from the passage above during compression:

"{dropped_sentence_text}"

Would removing this sentence make it impossible to answer a typical factual question about the
passage that a reader could otherwise answer using the full passage? Answer with exactly one word,
"yes" or "no", on the first line, then a single short sentence giving one reason on the second
line. Do not output anything else.
```
Temperature = 0. A "yes" answer is a **black-box disagreement flag**: round 1 (selection) and
round 2 (single-sentence-focused critique) disagree about that sentence's importance, and it
should be surfaced as a candidate for re-inclusion or manual review, not silently overridden.

**Prior art search — "self-critique to recover dropped content" / "reflection-based prompt
compression" / "self-refine compression" 2024–2026.** Scholarly and general searches for these
terms returned no paper that implements exactly this two-round drop-then-critique protocol for
extractive prompt compression. The closest related mechanism found is CompAct's (2407.09014)
**iterative** compression loop, which evaluates a *running summary* against the question and can
add back previously-omitted content in a subsequent iteration ("Query-focused Compression... If
certain sentences provide relevant information or implicitly clarify ambiguous points within the
question, the LLM is instructed to generate these sentences from the provided documents" —
sentence-level selection re-run over iterations), and Referential Dangling (2608.04569, see Q4)
which diagnoses *why* naive independent per-sentence selection breaks (missing bridge
dependencies) and evaluates a fixed **reselection** (not self-critique) repair. Neither is a
one-sentence-at-a-time "would removing this make the passage unanswerable" self-critique probe.
**No directly matching prior work found — state so explicitly; this appears to be a novel
combination**, built from independently verified components (CompAct's iterative re-inclusion
mechanism + ERASER's comprehensiveness-style counterfactual framing, see Q4c).
