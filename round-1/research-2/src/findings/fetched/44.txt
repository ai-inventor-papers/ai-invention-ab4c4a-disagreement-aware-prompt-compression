Can Large Language Models Explain Themselves?
A Study of LLM-Generated Self-Explanations

Abstract—Large language models (LLMs) such as ChatGPT
have demonstrated superior performance on a variety of natural
language processing (NLP) tasks including sentiment analysis,
mathematical reasoning and summarization. Furthermore, since
these models are instruction-tuned on human conversations
to produce “helpful” responses, they can and often will pro-
duce explanations along with the response, which we call self-
explanations. For example, when analyzing the sentiment of a
movie review, the model may output not only the positivity of the
sentiment, but also an explanation (e.g., by listing the sentiment-
laden words such as “fantastic” and “memorable” in the review).
How good are these automatically generated self-explanations?
In this paper, we investigate this question on the task of sentiment
analysis and for feature attribution explanation, one of the most
commonly studied settings in the interpretability literature (for
pre-ChatGPT models). Specifically, we study different ways to
elicit the self-explanations, evaluate their faithfulness on a set of
evaluation metrics, and compare them to traditional explanation
methods such as occlusion or LIME saliency maps. Through
an extensive set of experiments, we find that ChatGPT’s self-
explanations perform on par with traditional ones, but are quite
different from them according to various agreement metrics,
meanwhile being much cheaper to produce (as they are generated
along with the prediction). In addition, we identified several
interesting characteristics of them, which prompt us to rethink
many current model interpretability practices in the era of
ChatGPT(-like) LLMs.
Index Terms—Machine Learning, Natural Language Process-

I. INTRODUCTION
Over the past couple years, instruction-tuned [30] large lan-
guage models (LLMs) have revolutionized the field of natural
language processing (NLP) with its superior performance on a
wide variety of tasks and benchmarks such as sentiment analy-
sis [44], mathematical reasoning [14] and summarization [48].
Commercial models such as ChatGPT and GPT-4 by OpenAI,
Bard by Google and Claude by Anthropic have been used
to (partially) automate many human labor-intensive processes
such as education [22], trip planning [46] and customer service
[15]. A notable feature of these models is the ability to provide
(seemingly) reasonable explanations. For example, when asked
to solve a math problem, they often include detailed derivation
steps even when not explicitly instructed to do so. Similarly,
when asked to analyze the sentiment of a movie or book
review, they often spontaneously explain their decision with
supporting evidence, such as by highlighting sentiment-laden
words or explaining the sarcasm in the text.

How should we think about these explanations? In par-
ticular, do these explanations faithfully support the model’s
prediction (e.g., the final answer of the math problem or the
positive/negative judgment in sentiment analysis), or do they
simply give a sense of transparency while being decoupled
from the prediction? To what extent are they similar to or
different from the explanations generated from traditional
techniques [e.g. 24, 25, 28, 34, 37, 49], which are commonly
used to study predecessor models such as BERT [12] and GPT-
2 [33]? For any “negative results” about the effectiveness of
these explanations, should we attribute them to the model’s
inability to generate the explanations, or the limitations in our
evaluations [13, 23] about them?
In this paper, we seek answers to the above questions, using
OpenAI’s ChatGPT as the model, feature attributions as the
explanation type and sentiment analysis as the task. All three
design choices are made based on popularity in the respective
aspect: ChatGPT is arguably the most widely used instruction-
tuned LLM, feature attribution (also known as saliency map
or feature importance) explanation is by far the most popular

type of explanations studied in the interpretability literature,
and sentiment analysis is often the first task to consider when
benchmarking these explanations due to the intuitive alignment
between strong sentiment words (e.g, “fantastic” or “terrible”)
with feature attribution scores.
In the experiment, we study two paradigms of generating
the explanations: generating the explanation first before the
prediction (explain-then-predict or E-P), versus generating the
prediction first and then explaining it (predict-and-explain
or P-E) [7]. In each paradigm, we construct two methods
to eliciting the explanations: asking the model to generate
a full list of feature attribution explanations containing the
importance score of every word, and asking the model to just
return the top-k most important words. We then compare them
with two classic interpretability methods, occlusion [25] and
LIME [34]. The comparison is done on two fronts, a suite of
faithfulness evaluation metrics [51] and a set of disagreement
measurements among explanations [23].
Quantitatively, the model’s self-explanations perform on par
with traditional methods via faithfulness evaluations, but at the
same time differ greatly from them (on various agreement met-
rics). More importantly, we find that the classic interpretability
pipeline of defining and evaluating model explanations may be
fundamentally ill-suited for these LLMs with quite human-like
reasoning ability. Specifically, the model does not produce suf-
ficiently fine-grained model predictions for us to meaningfully
compute and evaluate explanations, in ways that we have been
done for predecessor models such as BERT [12]. Nonetheless,
we believe that this should be considered as a limitation on
the current interpretability practice, rather than the model,
as many such computations (e.g., identifying the effect of
removing a single word in a long sentence) are ill-posed for
humans as well. Overall, we hope that this work could bring
formal investigations of LLM-generated self-explanations to
the attention of the community, and inspire more work towards
better understanding and using them.
II. RELATED WORK
Our research lies at the intersection of three areas within
interpretability research: feature attribution explanations, eval-
uations of feature attribution explanations and LLM-generated
self-explanations. The former two areas are well-studied, so
we give emphasis to works that are directly used in our paper.
For broader contexts and more comprehensive coverage, we
refer the reader to the textbook by Molnar [29]. The last area is
relatively new, and while, to the best of our knowledge, we are
the first to rigorously study LLM-generated feature attribution
explanations, we briefly discuss some related ideas.
A. Feature Attribution Explanations
At a high level, feature attribution explanations assign
attribution scores to input features [52]. There are in general
two ways of defining input features. The first is based on
the model’s response to infinitesimal perturbation of the input
feature value, with representative methods such as the gradient
saliency [37], smooth gradient [39] and integrated gradient
[41]. For NLP models, the perturbation is often done in the
embedding space [24]. However, since ChatGPT is a closed-
source model that does not provide input gradients, none of
these explanations are possible to calculate.
Instead, we focus on the second approach: defining feature
importance based on a model’s response to inputs with various
features removed. The simplest definition is the occlusion
saliency [25, 49], with the importance of a feature (i.e., word)
defined as the change in the model prediction on the sentence
with that word removed. To capture non-linear interactions
among different words, LIME [34] and SHAP [28] remove
multiple features at the same time and define feature impor-
tance as linear regression coefficients or the game-theoretic
Shapley value [35] respectively. The word removal strategy
can be influential, with Pham et al. [31] finding that some
strategies favor some evaluations. In this paper, we adopt the
mainstream approach of word deletion (i.e., removing the word
“This” from the sentence “This is a great movie” results in “is
a great movie”), and leave the exploration of other strategies
(e.g., mask infilling) to future work.
B. Evaluations of Feature Attribution Explanations
Our main contribution of this paper is an investigation
into the relative strengths and weaknesses of LLM-generated
self-explanations compared to traditional explanations such as
occlusion and LIME. In this paper, we evaluate both their
faithfulness and agreement with each other.
For faithfulness evaluations, we employ several popular
metrics, including comprehensiveness [13], sufficiency [13],
decision flip rate under the removal of the most important
token/word [10], fraction of tokens need to be removed for
decision flip [36] and deletion rank correlation [3]. They all
subscribe to a fundamental principle – modifying a significant
feature, as identified by the explanation, should markedly
influence the prediction – but implement it differently.
Furthermore, recognizing that there is no “ground truth”
model reasoning available (due to the black-box nature of
these models), one type of evaluation modifies the dataset and
retrains the model [1, 2, 6, 47, 52], such that the model has
to use a specific type of reasoning for high accuracy, and then
evaluates whether the saliency maps of such high-accuracy
models align with the expectation. Given the high cost and
limited flexibility (API access only) of finetuning, we leave
these evaluations to future work.
We also study the correlation between the LLM self-
explanation and traditional techniques using the set of dis-
agreement metrics introduced by Krishna et al. [23]. Addi-
tional manifestation of the disagreement problem includes the
finding that the explanations themselves are often unstable [16]
and that they are very sensitive to hyperparameter choices [5].
C. LLM-Generated Self-Explanations
Generative models, those that can generate texts such as
the GPT family of models [32, 33], offer a distinct way
of explaining them – by simply generating the explanations
directly. Camburu et al. [7] trained models on supervised

pairs of labels and explanations and considered two orderings
of prediction and explanation: making a prediction and then
explaining it, and generating an explanation and using it to
make a prediction. We study both approaches in our paper. In
addition, Camburu et al. [7] found that training the model to
generate the explanation, both before and after the prediction,
results in a slight accuracy drop, casting doubt on the inherent
usefulness of the explanation to the prediction.
For ChatGPT and later models, chain-of-thought generation
[45], which can be considered as the model’s explanation, is
often helpful for the accuracy of the final answer, especially on
complex reasoning tasks such as solving math problems [18].
This evidence implies that the model could meaningfully rea-
son from such explanations. By contrast, [8] studied properties
of self-generated counterfactual explanations, proposed several
metrics and found that these explanations are often misleading
and lead to the wrong mental model of human inspectors.
III. METHODOLOGY
Our main contribution in this paper is a suite of systematic
analyses of LLM-generated self-explanations in the sentiment
analysis domain. We first covers the basics of auto-regressive
LLMs (such as ChatGPT and similar conversational models),
and then describe our prompting strategy and considerations.
After that, we introduce the two traditional interpretability
methods that we compare to, and also evaluation metrics.
A. Auto-regressive LLMs
Auto-regressive LLMs, as pioneered by GPT-1 [32] and
GPT-2 [33], operate by successively generating the next word
(or token) given the history (called context window), until
a special stop token is generated. The generation can be
stochastic or deterministic, and various decoding strategies
could be used to improve the quality of the generation [19]. In
all of our experiments, we use deterministic greedy decoding
(i.e., with temperature = 0). Thus, the generation process can
be modeled as the function
xg = g(xc),
(1)
where xc is the sequence of context words, and xg is the
sequence of generated words.
A conversational LLM (i.e., an LLM that can engage in
human conversations) typically has three message types: an
initial “system” message to set the general tone and instruction
of the conversation, followed by rounds of interleaving “user”
and “assistant” messages. While the exact details of ChatGPT
is unknown due to its proprietary nature, similar open-source
models such as Alpaca [42] and Vicuna [9] all adopt the same
practice to represent the message history: by concatenating
individual message prepended with the role indicator.
For example, if the model (i.e., assistant) needs to answer
the user’s second question below:

B. Prompting
Our central goal is to have the model generate a predic-
tion on the sentiment of the input sentence and a feature
attribution explanation for the prediction. We include both
explanation pipeline setups studied by Camburu et al. [7],
explain-then-predict (E-P), in which the model first generates
the explanation and then arrive at a prediction based on it, and
predict-and-explain (P-E), in which the model first generates
the prediction and then explains it.
Our prompts for E-P and P-E setups are shown in Table I
and II respectively. In the design of both prompts, we have
several considerations. First, in order to minimize the number
of turns in the conversation, we instruct the model to output
both the explanation and prediction at the same time, but in
clearly defined ordering. Second, in some of our experiments,
we need to feed the model with partial inputs (i.e., sentences
with some words removed) such as “This is a really great
movie with beautiful music and intricate plot” ⇒“This a
really movie with music and intricate”. We empirically find
that models without any guidance sometimes will refuse to
provide an answer, so we intentionally ask them to give their
best guess despite input ambiguity. Finally, we initially had
significant difficulty making the model to output the word
attribution values in a consistent format, but in the end find that
the “Python” format works the best for the model to follow.
Despite the success in generating syntactically correct out-
puts, a concern with this Python format guiding is that the
unnaturalness of output (compared to, e.g., Table III) may
result in the explanation values also being less faithful to
the model’s true reasoning. Thus, to remove any effect of the
unnatural prompt, and recognizing that humans, to which these
models are aligned [30], are not be able to produce detailed
attribution scores, we additionally consider two top-k versions
of the E-P and P-E setups. We construct these prompts to be as
natural as possible while ensuring that the result remains easy
to parse, and the final prompts are shown in Table IV and V
respectively. The number k is chosen dynamically according to
each input sentence as min(1, ⌊L/5⌋), where L is the number
of words in the sentence.

C. Traditional Interpretability Methods
We benchmark the LLM-generated self-explanations with
traditional interpretability methods that are often used to
explain classifiers. As mentioned in Section II-A, gradient-
based attribution values cannot be used due to the lack of
input gradient access. Instead, we consider two perturbation-
based methods, occlusion [25] and LIME [34]. The occlusion
method defines the attribution value on word w as
a(w) = f(x) −f(x−w),
(2)
where f(·) is the model prediction (as the generated decimal
point number in the range of 0.0 to 1.0) for the input sentence,
x is the original sentence, and x−w is the input sentence with
the word w removed. Intuitively, the occlusion value captures
the effect of a single word removal on model prediction.
By comparison, LIME recognizes that the effect of word
removal may not be linearly additive (i.e., the total prediction
change from removing two words is not the sum of prediction
changes from removing each word), and thus proposes to
remove random subsets of words, computes the model pre-
diction change, and attributes the change to individual words
by solving a linear regression problem.

decision flip (DFFrac) [36] and rank correlation with model

E. A Caveat on Model Sameness
Since the model is auto-regressive in nature, its output is
affected by two factors: the model weight and the context (i.e.,
the prompt). Thus, given the same model (e.g., ChatGPT),
prompting it in different ways (e.g., via different system
messages) effectively leads to different models. Thus, in our
experiment, we need to treat the E-P and P-E prompt as
two different models, and the generated self-explanations
are not comparable to each other. In addition, in order to
make meaningful comparisons between self-explanation and
occlusion/LIME, we need the latter to be generated with the
same system prompt, too.
Concretely, as an example, for the sentence “I really like this
movie”, to obtain the occlusion value for the word “like” that
is comparable to E-P self-explanation, we need to compute
the model prediction difference on the input “I really like this
movie” vs. “I really this movie”, both using the prompt as
shown in Table I, even though we do not need the explanation
value for the latter sentence. The same reasoning applies
on the evaluation side, where in order to retrieve the model
prediction values, we need to use the original prompt and
ignore the explanation output byproduct.
This also means that the top-k E-P and P-E explanations
are not those for the E-P and P-E models. However, there
is no way around this issue: in order for the model to
generate the top-k explanation, it has to be instructed in that
way, which effectively results in a different model. Thus, in
our study, we ignore the difference, and consider the top-
k explanation effectively as an explanation generated by an
external explainer, for the target E-P or P-E model.

C. Faithfulness Evaluation Results
Table VII presents the performance of SELFEXP for E-P
and P-E models, compared to occlusion and LIME generated
for each model respectively. We make several remarks below.
First, we note the lack of a clear winner across the different
metrics. Unexpectedly, we see that the occlusion saliency
performs the best on DFMIT, because its definition (i.e., the
importance of a word defined as the model prediction change
under word removal) inherently makes its most important
word being the most likely to flip the model prediction when
removed – an observation of solvability made by Zhou and
Shah [51]. We should also expect it to achieve the first place
on RankDel, but all the numbers are very close to 0 and, as we
will show in Section IV-E, the insensitivity of model prediction
under single word removal makes this metric highly prone to
the effect of stochasticity in tie-breaking.
Among the remaining metrics, occlusion is the best on
none, which is not surprising as it could not capture the
non-linear effects of feature removals. Such non-linear effects
are considered by LIME, which performs quite evenly with
SELFEXP.
Nonetheless, running LIME is highly expensive: since it
needs to solve a linear regression problem, the default number
of perturbations by the Python lime package1 is 5,000,
meaning that we need to query the OpenAI API 5,000 times
in order to generate the explanation for a single sentence.
In practice, we adaptively scale this number to be 10 times
the number of tokens in the sentence with an average of 183
perturbations per explanation, but this still is quite expensive,
taking approximately 20 minutes per explanation.
Thus, given that SELFEXP performs on par with LIME, we
see that it could be low-cost substitute of LIME, as this self-
explanation is generated along with the prediction.

valid explanation and prediction, we are concerned that the

D. Agreement Evaluation Results
Figure 3 shows the agreement among various explanations,
for both the E-P and P-E setups. Similar to the findings by
[23], high disagreement among every pair of explanations
is exhibited. However, the faithfulness evaluation does not
identify any method as a top performer across the board, but
instead all methods perform very closely to each other. This is
in stark contrast with traditional interpretability methods gen-
erated for classifiers, where the performance difference among
different methods is usually much larger (c.f., results by Zhou
and Shah [51]). In other words, while Krishna et al. [23] found
that explanations that perform very differently on faithfulness
metrics (for classifier models) have high disagreement, we find
that explanations that perform similarly on faithfulness metrics
also have high disagreement (for the ChatGPT model).

E. Qualitative Analyses
During our experiments, we notice several differences be-
tween the explanation generation and evaluation for ChatGPT
versus those for other models. These differences often stem
from fundamental differences between ChatGPT and other
models, and serve as important caveats when generating and
understanding these explanations.
1) Level of Saliency Values: In the SELFEXP attribution
values, it is very common for them to be on a few levels,
whose values are often “well-rounded” decimal numbers such
as 0.5 and 0.75, rather than arbitrary ones such as 0.6423 and
0.1982 (often produced by traditional techniques for traditional
models). One example is shown in Table I and Table II, and
additional instances are presented in Table IX. Quantitatively,
on average each sentence has 18.2 words, but there are only
2.72 levels of unique explanations per sentence for the E-P
model and 5.69 for the P-E model.

We believe that this phenomenon occurs due to the human-
like behavior and reasoning capability of ChatGPT. In its
attempt to mimic a human (which is the central goal of
the reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)
alignment [30]), it outputs explanation values that are plausibly
produced by humans, who will not give very fine-grained
saliency values (e.g., 0.6423 and 0.1982) as these values are
often beyond the human’s reasoning ability.
As recent papers have noted [50, 53], model explanations
are often less understandable to humans than desirable in that
humans may not be able to draw the correct high-level conclu-
sions about the model’s reasoning from individual (i.e., local)
explanations. One potential culprit could be the traditionally
very fine-grained and varied saliency values, even if they
faithfully reflect the model’s reasoning. Thus, the ChatGPT
self-explanation may offer a different kind of faithfulness-
understandability trade-off, where the explanations are inten-
tionally vague but easier for human consumption. Testing this
hypothesis requires a formal evaluation of understandability
with human subjects, which we leave to future work. Or-
thogonally, comparing the explanations generated by aligned
and unaligned models could identify the impact of alignment,
though getting access to the latter model may prove difficult
as companies are often reluctant to release these models due
to safety concerns [20].
We attempted to combat the issue with few-shot prompting,
where we provide some explanation values according to our
intuitions on some examples before asking the model to
generate its own explanation and prediction. However, the
results are less than satisfactory. Few-shot prompting does
solve the issue of saliency values being too “rounded:” when
provided with more fine-grained values (e.g. 0.8694 and -
0.1285) as attribution scores, the generated explanations also
tend to be less rounded. However, still not too many saliency
levels are present. In addition, ChatGPT tends to “memorize”
the provided attribution values for specific words. For example,
when the word “the” has saliency value of 0.163 in the
exemplar, the model is likely to assign the same value of
0.163 to other instances of “the” in the query sentence. A full
example demonstrating both phenomena is presented in Table
X. As a result of the weaknesses of the few-shot prompting,
we opted to base our experiments on the more stable, human-
like responses from the zero-shot prompting setup. Overall,
our findings suggest that the model is learning to mimic the
human-provided explanation, which is a fundamentally flawed
behavior: we want the model’s explanation to reflect its own
reasoning process, rather than reflecting the reasoning process
of another decision maker (human or model).

with certain words removed. Recall that since we do not have
access to token probability output from ChatGPT, we define
the model prediction as the textual output of the confidence
score as a floating point number. Since this textual output is
also shaped by alignment, we have similar findings as before.
